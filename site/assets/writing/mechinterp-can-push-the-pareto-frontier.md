# Mechinterp Can Push the Pareto Frontier

November 24, 2026

Take everything I write here with a pound of salt. I’m just exploring and I’m not entirely sure if the definitions, assumptions, and suggestions I make in this article are accurate. It also doubles as a reflection of my current understanding as I finish my final semester of high school and prepare for a variable-length career in AI. I’ve used ChatGPT and Claude to review what I’ve written and give suggestions, and Codex to confirm citations, but this is an important message and these words are ultimately my own. Happy to update my priors.

## Definitions:

Mechanistic Interpretability (mechinterp): building a rigorous, verified understanding of model behavior through its internals (hence “mechanistic”). This is a general, imperfect definition for the set of useful activities within mechanistic interpretability.

Pareto frontier: the set of achievable states for which no variable in an objective function can be increased without decreasing another. When I reference the Pareto frontier in this essay, I mean both the model-level and system-level frontier.

Alignment: A model’s adherence to a set of principles or axioms. Alignment can be anything but is most discussed in the context of helpfulness and harmlessness towards humans: a common example is Asimov’s Three Laws of Robotics. Philosophical definitions of what alignment should be differ by school of thought and objective function.

Pre/post-hoc alignment: when in the model’s training process alignment is introduced (during training, after training). This can vary, and a more precise definition ([pre|mid|post|zero-]training alignment, or naming specific mechanisms) is more helpful. For one example of this, read my John Locke Essay ([linked here](https://www.tarush.ai/writing/should-we-be-polite-to-chatgpt)).

AI Safety: (for the purposes of this writeup, definitions differ) the field surrounding the definition of conventional alignment and its practical execution in deployed AI systems

AGI: artificial general intelligence. Capable of doing everything a human can do at the level of a 50th percentile person, not just reasoning but also interacting with the physical world and beyond.

ASI: artificial superintelligence. In my opinion, a category, not an individual system. It describes the capacity for an artificial intelligence to supersede all humans.

SASI: specialized artificial superintelligence. Capable of outperforming every human being in a specific field.

GASI: generalized artificial superintelligence. Capable of outperforming every human being at EVERYTHING a human can do. (This is what many people call ASI; I feel the “general” component is important here).

I recognize many of these definitions may be incongruous with yours or the field’s; please attempt to read my essay in the context I’ve defined, or adjust the reference point to your definitions (can be trivially done with AI).

## TLDR

While AI safety and alignment work is critical, I believe that mechanistic interpretability has immense applications beyond it, and it should pay for itself as a research direction not just by aligning models but unlocking other benefits of understanding them, many of which are not visible ahead of building rigorous understanding. The most relevant one that I’ve considered so far is that it has the potential to push the capability-compute Pareto frontier of AI systems. I’m fascinated by whether scalable, machine-native interpretability can change how models learn across various training phases (pre/mid/post training, test-time training, continual learning, etc.), reason, epistemically calibrate their own uncertainty, and work collectively towards a common goal. Here, I explore why I think this form of mechinterp is valuable post-RSI, and why I consider it Bitter Lesson compatible.

## RSI and my understanding of our current timeline.

It seems RSI is in flight. OpenAI and Anthropic have publicly discussed their research acceleration and how pretraining gains are causing massive capability uptakes. Google DeepMind is rumored to use internal looped mechanisms to improve model performance (this is a rumor, not confirmed fact). Whether these processes can be honestly defined as recursive self improvement is debatable. However, this is a stated objective for frontier labs with countless immensely capable researchers, and I have confidence that we will eventually converge on an RSI or RSI-adjacent system. I am also confident that this system will produce post-human intelligence in narrow verticals. I’d argue that multiple SASIs will arrive before AGI or GASI. I also think that the most credible approach to arriving at posthuman systems is RSI or RSI-adjacent.

I believe that we are exceptionally close to SASI right now. If you define some performance function P(time, compute, available data, environment/tooling, human assistance)=performance (a real performance function may be substantially different), for many verticals (i.e. math, coding), models, given the same inputs (although this is hard to quantify in humans), will produce outputs that the best human coder or mathematician cannot produce. I believe we do not have SASI yet because we have not seen the same post-frontier-level results from an undirected SASI without a μ+3𝜎 human guiding it. Perhaps there are SASIs internally to frontier labs that I’ve never encountered, and this timeline may need to be updated.

## What happens post-SASI?

Having a SASI-level system does not mean that humans cannot still help. Presumably, humans sourcing training data or suggesting problems may still be important, for a while. But taken to its limit, we may not understand what problems are being solved, just that the problems are exceptionally important and nontrivial, and may result in concrete impacts on our lives. Or not. At what point do humans accept that there is no way for us to help? Who collaborates with a post-human agent? How do agents know when they are wrong? This is partially answered by formal verification. Lean is based on certain invariant principles of mathematics that may not break unless those axioms are violated. What about nondeterministic advancement? What if models converge on the idea that every human philosophy is inaccurate given the principles of the universe, and we should all update our religious beliefs? We don’t have a Nietzsche-kernel.

How do we introduce epistemic humility into something smarter than us, many of whose answers we cannot verify as correct with any simulation? [Jev by TypeSafe AI](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)’s principle is probabilistic calibration. You don’t need an autoregressive behemoth to tell you which language model to choose or which simple decision to make. What happens when you do? Can we ever have deterministic model self consistency in nondeterministic single and multi-agent model systems? When conducting frontier research, human researchers continually update their priors based on experimental evidence.

Counterfactual self-consistency training (confirming self-consistency without blatant scenario memorization or superficial assumptions) without compromising on raw capability in pretraining is a particularly compelling answer. If the native model substrate learns epistemic humility, it can go in a different direction entirely. But how can we be sure that this counterfactual training will maintain the Pareto frontier? Or any training intervention, for that matter. A change in the gradients or a deviation from how models are trained today has to be justified in a preservation or improvement of capability at the same compute cost. Perhaps it is possible that the training does not negatively impact capability. Then how do we build that into an RSI-native paradigm that is innately-self referential? How do humans know that this is pushing the Pareto frontier? Why should we deviate from the existing paradigm that scales well, unless we find something that scales better?

## Enter mechinterp.

Ironically, I’d argue that mechinterp is the direction that pushes the Pareto frontier and makes post-RSI systems epistemically self consistent enough to explore directions reasonably and compute optimally without continuous external epistemic supervision. Why is this ironic? Because by many scaling researchers, mechinterp is seen as a waste of time and compute that is justified only because of potential AI safety benefits. Existing paradigms like probes and SAEs are expensive to train for immense frontier systems, and many times don’t buy you control over model internals. Developmental interpretability (exploring how internal representations change and computational components emerge in training) is similarly expensive, requiring per-n checkpoint intervention and evaluation. We need versions of interpretability systems that are either so trivially cheap to train and run that we can use them incredibly frequently in training, or generalize (either natively or with orthogonal training objectives) over weight-shift while still interpreting them accurately. With recent work by [Cammarata and others at Goodfire](https://www.goodfire.com/research/neurons-as-an-interpretable-basis/paper.pdf) on neurons as an interpretable basis, I’d argue that we’re closer to that than before. Superposition is still real and the most compute-intensive way to explain it is to decompress it to a higher-dimensional space, but there may be ways to tolerate a phase shift to the alien representations of a language model without attempting to impose human-linguistic concepts to them. [Anthropic research released yesterday (November 23, 2026) on neurons as an interpretable basis](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-theory/) provides preliminary support for several of these intuitions (heavy emphasis on preliminary). Native neurons outperform tested SAE baselines on AxBench concept detection, neuron outputs exhibit greater interpretability than activation analyses suggested with an approximately fixed output basis across layers. [Neurons demonstrate practical utility in detection and steering](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2026/neuron-basis-applications/), and there is evidence of neuron function reuse in fine tuning. While these results still need to be reproduced (I have that actively in flight as I write this essay today), this is promising. These readouts rely on the J lens, which my initial research found can shift with fitting corpus (although I ran experiments on smaller models of a different family, and generalizability to this isn’t particularly established). Regardless, I believe that there are ways to trivially interpret these systems that we’ve not yet explored because we’ve taken a human-native approach to interpretability. [Weight-space programming](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/) could be an example that models may be better at interpreting one another than we are, although it’s still underexplored. [Subliminal learning](https://alignment.anthropic.com/2025/subliminal-learning/) also hints at this: a model’s preference (e.g. a fondness for owls) can transfer to another model through sequences of plain numbers that look meaningless to humans, even after filtering. This specific effect needs the two models to share the same base model, but it suggests that model outputs carry signals that a human-native approach to mechinterp may miss.

In all honesty, I think that the ideal model system (keep in mind that this is different from the ones that we may actually be able to verify and deploy) will allow models to reason entirely in their native substrate and introduce scalable checkpoint-invariant and verifiable interpretability systems that epistemically calibrate their uncertainty without demanding training interventions to make model representations more human-readable. This is, in my opinion, far more Pareto-minded than counterfactually training models to interpret them more easily, or relying entirely on complicated external training signals to epistemically calibrate models that are busy continually learning over post-human-complexity problems and searching for a solution under immense optimization self-pressure.

## Beyond epistemic uncertainty.

Let’s say we decide that epistemic uncertainty isn’t as important. Behavioral verification, by SASI or GASI systems leaving a path that verifiers can retrace, becomes easier. Teams of exceptional humans, AIs, or both are somehow able to span the massive space of second-order effects of each monumental discovery or output produced by a model at the same rate that new ones are produced. I’d argue that ideal behavioral verification is far more computationally and time-expensive than ideal mechinterp wrt future discoveries, but assume that it isn’t. Are we back to square one? Is mechinterp now only useful for niche AI safety bets that don’t scale again?

I don’t think so. Call me optimistic, but I believe that it’s at least worth considering that analysis of the internal computations of the model, when taken to its limit, may scale directly with the model as it trains and beyond. One example of this is interpretable or semi-interpretable harness engineering. Another is adaptive inference through early-exits, which can and does benefit from tools like the logit/tuned lenses and activation patching. Furthermore, distillation to smaller, optimized expert models (for instance, converting a single model into a multi-agent orchestration system) has an active subfield in mechanistic distillation. Mechinterp training-native interventions that actually improve post-training performance, or more optimized loss functions that target the minimal components of a network that need to be calculated are still being explored. [Weight-space programming](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/) and practical autointerpretability as a proxy for training, as they are substantially refined in the future, can even operate as single-turn equivalences of training methods. Mechinterp can even potentially model performance relationships for upscaling architectural components of transformers in training. All of these are fascinating and potentially completely inaccurate intuitions and moonshots. However, understanding a system, even if understanding is substantially elusive, can potentially buy us many things, and I don’t think we should be too fast to dismiss mechinterp or put it in an AI-safety shaped box. I also want to emphasize that calling this form of mechinterp anti-Bitter Lesson pilled is, in my opinion, a misunderstanding of the Bitter Lesson. If anything, I’d argue mechinterp has the potential to be one of the most Bitter-Lesson pilled disciplines in AI.

## My research/work wrt this

My existing research connects to many of the results I’ve discussed above. The Anthropic neuron-basis work reads each neuron’s output through the Jacobian lens (or J lens for short), and the authors point out that interpretation depends on the readout. In [The Fitting Corpus Is Part of the Measurement](https://www.tarush.ai/research/fittingcorpus/) (NeurIPS ‘26 InterpScience workshop), I audit a J lens on Pythia 410M, 1B, and 2.8B. I find that the fitting corpus can change reconstruction performance with model and evaluation held consistent (See [tarush.ai/research/fitting-corpus](https://www.tarush.ai/research/fittingcorpus/) for more information). Model scale and use of the lens between these two scenarios differ and my findings may not be generalizable, but regardless, auditing the sensitivity of interpretability tools is an important step towards eventually using interpretability beyond AI safety applications.

Going forward, I’m particularly interested in computationally efficient interpretability, the evolution of model representations in training, and the possibility of more invariant, robust, compute-inexpensive interpretability tools or methods. Ultimately, I don’t just want to conduct frontier research on this. I also want to explore whether these methods can become useful primitives in training, inference, eventually continually learning, posthuman single and multi-agent systems; I also want to find concrete ways to apply this correctly in real environments and applications. I’m excited to explore whether my theses about the Pareto frontier are right, and update my beliefs about what I find.
