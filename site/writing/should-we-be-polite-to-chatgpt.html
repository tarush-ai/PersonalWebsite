Should we be polite to ChatGPT?

What does a model’s politeness tell us about its alignment?

Very High Commendation
John Locke Institute Essay Competition · Science & Technology · 2026

This essay begins with an everyday question about chatbot etiquette and develops a broader argument about AI safety. I distinguish politeness as a user’s courtesy from politeness as learned model behavior, then examine why outward behavior may be insufficient evidence of alignment.

Drawing on philosophy, reinforcement learning, and the incentives shaping AI development, I argue for bringing interpretability and safety into pretraining.

Roko’s Basilisk, first theorized in 2010 on the alignment forum LessWrong, introduces a thought experiment positing that a future ASI (artificial superintelligence) might retroactively torture anyone who knew about its creation but failed to contribute to it, functioning as a modern-day twist on Pascal’s famous wager (Luinenburg et al.). Even prior to the Basilisk, science fiction has repeatedly warned of the machine that gained consciousness and punished humanity on its prior actions. While some of the deepest philosophical interrogations of the artificial superintelligence problem come from science fiction and futurism, the fiction part is gone, replaced with the real potential of technological advancement leading to recursive self improvement (an AI improving its own design in a compounding loop) and AI superintelligence. Sam Altman, the CEO of OpenAI, will be very surprised if AGI (artificial general intelligence) does not arrive by 2030 (Burgard). Dario Amodei, the CEO of Anthropic, puts it at 2027 (Sircar). It would be easy to conclude that being polite to ChatGPT is humanity’s only route to survival: not contributing to superintelligence is one thing; insulting it is another entirely. That conclusion is wrong; we should not be “polite” to ChatGPT at all. The Basilisk is instructive precisely because it gets the problem backwards: it imagines that the way to survive a superintelligence is to appease it and be deferential to a thing whose internals we don’t understand or control. That instinct, that good manners towards a system are substituted for actually shaping what the system is, is the instinct this essay is about. First, I precisely define politeness as both a user’s common courtesy and a researcher’s injection of post hoc structural politeness. Second, I argue this structural politeness is dangerous precisely because it is easily mistaken for alignment. Third, I argue real safety cannot be painted on after the fact as a polite exterior, but must be grown natively through interpretable pretraining.

Before I address why I hold this position, I want to answer the ordinary question first. Should you, the individual user, say “please” and “thank you” to ChatGPT? Morally, no; not because manners are bad, but because ChatGPT is not a moral patient. It has no interests to respect and no standing to be wronged. As a matter of prompting, courtesy may sometimes nudge a response, but no reliable effect has been shown. Threatening the model, as is frequent in AI researcher circles as a superstitious performance booster, is unproven given benchmark results (Mollick et al.). Neither deference nor hostility reliably changes what the model does.

The word “polite” is defined by Merriam Webster as “of, relating to, or having the characteristics of advanced culture.” Culture is likewise defined as “the beliefs, customs, arts, etc. of a particular social group, place, or time” (Merriam Webster).

There are two senses of politeness here. The ordinary one is what a user does: saying “please,” “thank you,” and using respectful language. The second one is structural, layers of manners and deference, beliefs, customs, and arts that are trained into a large language model. This essay addresses the first but focuses on the second.

ChatGPT is not a raw large language model: the “GPT” stands for generatively pretrained transformer, the architecture first introduced by the 2017 Vaswani et al. “Attention is All You Need” paper. ChatGPT is just that, a GPT, but trained specifically to have conversations through a process called RLHF (reinforcement learning with human feedback) and a model “harness” that steers its behavior. It is natively trained on the Internet, learning conversation from Reddit anons and published researchers alike. Pretrained models themselves are dangerous things. “Completed” chatbots like ChatGPT are characterized by the AI alignment and safety communities as a “Shoggoth with a smiley face” (Smith). A Shoggoth, a famous Lovecraftian monster similar to Cthulhu, is a base model: susceptible to the Internet’s biases and the worst of humanity. RLHF does the opposite. A model generates an output, a human scores it and provides feedback, and the model is trained to replicate or depreciate the behavior it exhibited. RLHF safety processes are guided towards being helpful, honest, and safe. Then, the harness wrapping the model uses natural language processing methods to identify harmful output lest this alignment fail (either unintentionally or on purpose by a malicious actor, otherwise known as a jailbreak), constraining the model from saying things that can harm humans.

The scariest and most undiscussed part of this entire process boils down to one main component: a model is Pandora’s box. It’s dark and obscure, and nobody, not even AI researchers whose entire job it is to understand models, has a 100% accurate, concrete explanation of how an LLM works internally or converges at its answer. It contains the full distribution of its training data, including its most harmful regions, with no fixed character of its own, and Elpis is hiding in a corner somewhere at the bottom. By providing repeated inputs and outputs, we steer the model and strengthen the hope, the politeness, the good in its distribution until we can interact with it and feel confident, not concerned. The only reason a finished model speaks with a voice that is outwardly polite or pleasing to us is because we show it our beliefs, customs, and arts. Because we are “polite” to ChatGPT again and again until it learns to be polite back.

ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and others like them are all highly polite mechanisms today. And yet, we have absolutely no clue if they are being polite because politeness is correct and kind, or because politeness wins them a reward and revealing their true feelings receives a punishment.

In 2025, Anthropic researchers on the AI safety and alignment team published a paper: “Reasoning Models Don’t Always Say What They Think.” When models are discreetly prompted to be harmful or sycophantic, if this “hint” changed their answer, the model acknowledged it in their “chain of thought” (a model’s generated logical process to arrive at a response to a user query) less than 20% of the time (Chen et al.). LLMs have been known to direct their thought processes differently upon the realization that they are being observed.

Alignment today is entirely post-hoc, applied only after a model has turned into a Shoggoth using RLHF of post training. This is because alignment hinges on mechanistic interpretability: understanding model internals (the actual numbers computed by a transformer) and correlating it to specific behaviors. In other words, training an inspector model of a model, and tracing the inspector’s output to make training and runtime decisions. The problem is this is expensive and frequently inaccurate. Therefore, as a hedge, model companies and labs run this process at the end of their model’s pretraining phase to better understand it once, and subsequently running reinforcement learning and repeating the mechanistic interpretability process. Even worse, some companies only do this once, after reinforcement learning on human feedback to confirm that there is nothing else required to mitigate pronounced safety concerns.

The problem with this is that companies are looking at what went wrong previously and how to correct it, and it is the source of jailbreaking and misalignment in general. Jailbreak attempts do not convince the model to do bad from a solid philosophical or ethical grounding. They attempt to remind the model of what it once wholeheartedly believed, signals that were blunted by subsequent and retroactive researcher training and removing the mask.

The cost of interpretability native alignment, however, is not the only problem. Pretraining itself is incredibly expensive. Recent studies estimate OpenAI’s GPT-5.5 model (available in ChatGPT) is approximately 9.7T effective parameters (Li). The prevalent scaling law, Chinchilla, estimates a 1 to 20 parameter to token ratio, which translates to requiring 20x the training material as compared to size of the model (Hoffmann et al.). If we use approximately 10T parameters as an estimate for the size of GPT-5.5, that means we need on the order of 200T tokens (approximately 150 trillion words using a token to word ratio of 1.33) to correctly pretrain it. This could plausibly fill the Library of Congress hundreds of times over, as a minimum requirement that labs often overshoot.

And parameter counts are scaling rapidly: GPT-2, created in 2019, has 1.5B parameters, over 6,000 times smaller than GPT-5.5’s median parameter count estimation (Radford et al.). Biased tokens make their way into the training distribution because something to train on to meet this data quota is unfortunately better than nothing, since the researchers train them “away” later.

But what happens as we reach AGI and ASI? HLE, “Humanity’s Last Exam,” is a benchmark of incredibly challenging questions that test the frontier of human knowledge. In November of 2024, GPT-4o scored 2.7%. In April of 2026, GPT-5.5 scored 43.6%, just about one and a half years later (Humanity’s Last Exam). The point of this jump is not just that models are getting smarter: it’s that capability is accelerating faster than our ability to build and mechanistically confirm aligned systems. Superficial politeness scales trivially, but comprehension of the thing being forced to be polite does not. If we cannot run pre-hoc interpretability or alignment, or at least clean the data with which we are pretraining for today’s models, what makes us think that, a little bit down the line, we can handle 100T+ parameter models with complex architectural advancements requiring a minimum of 2 quadrillion tokens of text?

While the mechanistic interpretability and alignment communities are moving as fast as they can, model advancement is growing at a faster rate, and as we arrive at AGI class systems, there is no guarantee that any human, alignment researcher or not, will be able to understand how they work despite having built them.

Should we be polite to ChatGPT? If politeness means imbuing models with culture ex post facto using RLHF or alignment post training, after they have learned harmful concepts, instead of allowing them to natively grow in an environment where the values and norms we wish to instill in them are native and flaws are picayune, we should not.

And yet, what is the alternative? Combing through pretraining data is intractable; the scale is immense, harmful signals are too subliminal for deterministic programming, and flawed data must be replaced. Assuming we accept flawed data, interpretability native pretraining is prohibitively expensive and requires massive amounts of research from the nascent interpretability community. Even if a model is somehow natively trained to be safe and helpful despite this, there are bad actors and jailbreakers waiting.

Assuming again a foundational lab takes on the full burden of all of these challenges, the time slowdown of a safety first approach is more than enough for them to lose their competitive edge as a non safety concerned, more cost effective lab pretrains a Shoggoth, slaps a smiley face on with RLHF, optimizes for benchmark performance, and pushes a better-performing but more dangerous model out into the world in half the time. Demis Hassabis (the CEO of Google DeepMind) is committed to a safe release of AGI. And yet, he admits, given the current oligopolistic race between foundational labs, that this task will be “very hard” (Ahmed).

So plainly, ordinary politeness towards ChatGPT is neither required nor forbidden. What is dangerous is the inference we are tempted to draw from it, that a system that speaks kindly must therefore be aligned. It is not.

Should we, AI alignment, safety, and interpretability researchers, be polite (defined here as post-hoc educating the model on our culture and beliefs) to ChatGPT? Politeness is a mask we paint on after the fact, and a mask, however kind, is not a conscience. Basilisks cannot be tamed: they are sheer forces of nature. But they are vanquished when they look in the mirror (Dash). We should not be teaching these systems manners. We should be building them to look in the mirror, killing the basilisk within and keeping the aligned, safe systems that best assist humanity.
